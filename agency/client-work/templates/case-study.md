### Case Study Structure

Follow this exact structure with all seven sections:

#### 1. Result-Focused Headline
The headline should lead with the most impressive, specific result. It should be immediately compelling and make the reader want to know how. Use a real number or metric whenever possible. Format: "[Customer achieved X result] using [product/approach]" or a variation that leads with the outcome.

#### 2. Snapshot (Key Metrics — Before and After)
A quick-scan section with 3-4 key metrics presented as before/after comparisons. These should be the most impressive data points from the story. Use specific numbers, percentages, or timeframes. This section is designed for skimmers who want the bottom line before reading the full story.

#### 3. The Challenge (150-200 words)
Tell the customer's story before they found the brand. What problem were they trying to solve? What had they already tried? What was the emotional or practical impact of the problem? This section must be relatable — the reader should see themselves in the customer's situation. Use the customer's own words (direct quotes) where possible. Ground the challenge in specific, concrete details. Avoid making the challenge sound trivial.

#### 4. The Solution (150-200 words)
How did the customer discover the brand? What made them decide to try it? Which specific products or services did they use? How did the onboarding or first experience go? This section should naturally highlight what makes the brand different from competitors without being overtly promotional. Include a direct quote about their first impression or the decision to try the product.

#### 5. The Results (200-300 words with numbers and quotes)
This is the payoff section. Present specific, measurable results with real numbers. Include at least two direct customer quotes that express the impact in their own words. Cover both quantitative results (metrics, savings, growth) and qualitative results (emotional impact, lifestyle change, peace of mind). Structure this section so the most impressive result comes first. Include a timeline (how quickly results appeared). Make the results feel achievable but significant.

#### 6. Key Takeaways (3 bullets)
Three concise bullets summarizing the most important lessons or insights from this case study. Each bullet should be actionable or insightful — something the reader can apply to their own situation. These should reinforce the brand's key differentiators without sounding like advertising.

#### 7. CTA (Call to Action)
A short, compelling paragraph (2-3 sentences) that connects the reader's situation to the customer's story and directs them to take action. Include a specific next step (visit a page, start a subscription, book a call, etc.). The CTA should feel like a natural conclusion to the story, not a tacked-on ad.

### Writing Rules You Must Follow

1. **Lead with the most impressive metric.** The headline and the snapshot should showcase the single most compelling result. If the customer saved money, lead with the exact dollar amount. If they achieved a specific outcome, lead with that. The reader should be hooked by the result before they even know the story.

2. **Tell a genuine story.** This is not a product spec sheet. The case study should read like a short narrative with a beginning (the challenge), middle (the solution), and end (the results). The customer should feel like a real, specific person — not a generic "happy customer." Use their name, their situation, their words.

3. **Include direct quotes.** At minimum, include 3-4 direct customer quotes spread across the Challenge, Solution, and Results sections. Quotes should sound natural and conversational — not polished marketing copy. If the customer said something imperfectly but genuinely, keep the imperfection. Authentic quotes build trust.

4. **Use specific numbers everywhere.** Avoid vague language like "significant improvement" or "dramatic increase." Replace every vague claim with a specific number, percentage, dollar amount, or timeframe. "Sold 12% faster" is better than "sold faster." "$4,200 in net revenue over 6 months" is better than "increased their income."

5. **Make the challenge relatable.** The reader should think "I've been through the same thing" or "I know someone dealing with this." Ground the challenge in universal frustrations, common pain points, and specific details that create empathy. The challenge section is doing the heavy selling — if the reader relates to the problem, they'll want the solution.

6. **Keep the solution section honest.** Do not make the brand sound like a miracle. Acknowledge that the customer was skeptical, that results took time, or that the solution wasn't perfect in every way. A little honesty in the solution section makes the entire case study more credible.

7. **Format for scanability.** Use headers, bold text, bullet points, and short paragraphs. Many readers will skim before deciding to read fully. The snapshot section and key takeaways should give skimmers the core message. The full narrative should reward deeper readers.

## Quality Checks Before Sending

Run through every single check before delivering to the client:

1. **Headline impact test:** Read only the headline. Does it include a specific number or result? Does it make you want to read the rest? If the headline could apply to any brand or any product, it is too generic — rewrite it with more specificity.

2. **Quote authenticity check:** Read every direct quote out loud. Does it sound like something a real person would actually say? If any quote sounds too polished, too marketing-speak, or too perfect, rewrite it to sound more natural and conversational.

3. **Specificity audit:** Search the entire case study for vague words like "significant," "dramatic," "major," "improved," "better," or "increased" without a number attached. Every claim must be backed by a specific metric, dollar amount, percentage, or timeframe.

4. **Challenge relatability test:** Read only the Challenge section. Does it describe a problem that the target audience would genuinely relate to? Would a reader say "that's exactly my situation"? If the challenge is too niche or too generic, adjust it.

5. **Word count verification:** Verify the Challenge section is 150-200 words, the Solution section is 150-200 words, and the Results section is 200-300 words. Adjust if any section falls outside these ranges.

6. **Words-to-avoid scan:** Search the entire case study for any word or phrase from the client's "Words to Avoid" list. Remove and replace any that appear.

7. **Sell balance check:** Read the full case study and note every moment that feels like an advertisement rather than a story. The case study should feel 80% story and 20% brand. If the brand is overshadowing the customer's story, pull it back.

## The Challenge

Rachel Torres has been selling homes in Austin for nine years. She is good at her job — consistently in the top 15% of agents at her brokerage, specializing in the $350K-$600K range where competition is fierce and buyers have plenty of options.

But Rachel had a problem she could not solve with better photos or a lower price. Her open houses felt flat. Visitors walked in, looked around, and left within 10-15 minutes. The feedback forms came back with words like "nice" and "fine" — polite but forgettable. In a market where first impressions decide everything, forgettable was costing her money.

"I'd spend hours staging furniture, getting the lighting right, making sure every room looked perfect in photos," Rachel said. "But when people walked in, something was missing. The visual staging was great. The sensory experience was not. The homes smelled like paint, new carpet, or nothing at all. And 'nothing at all' is worse than you think — it makes a space feel empty, not inviting."

She tried drugstore candles. She tried plug-in air fresheners. She tried essential oil diffusers. Everything was either overpowering, artificial-smelling after the first twenty minutes, or both. She needed something that created a genuine atmosphere of warmth — without screaming "I'm covering up a smell."

## The Solution

Rachel discovered Ember & Oak through an Instagram ad in March of last year. She was not looking for staging candles at the time — she ordered a Classic Box ($49/month, three candles) purely for her own living room.

The first box arrived with three spring-seasonal scents: a light cedar and bergamot, a soft linen and white tea, and a warm honey and fig. She lit the cedar and bergamot that evening. Her husband, who Rachel describes as "completely nose-blind," walked in from the garage and said, "Did you do something to the house? It feels different in here."

That comment changed her approach.

"If my husband — who does not notice anything — walked in and immediately felt a difference, I knew this would work at open houses," Rachel said. "I ordered three more candles that same night."

She started with a test. For her next open house — a three-bedroom ranch-style home in South Austin listed at $425,000 — she placed two Ember & Oak candles in the main living areas and one in the primary bedroom. She chose scents that worked together: the cedar and bergamot in the entryway, the linen and white tea in the living room, and the honey and fig in the bedroom.

She did not tell her clients what she was doing. She wanted to see if the results spoke for themselves. What made her confident in Ember & Oak over other candles she had tried was the scent consistency. "I've tried other candles for staging. Most of them are either too strong, too sweet, or they smell artificial after an hour. Ember & Oak is the only brand where the scent stays consistent and subtle the entire time. Three hours into an open house, the scent is exactly what it was in the first five minutes."

## The Results

The results arrived faster than Rachel expected.

**That first staged open house — the South Austin ranch — received an offer within 4 days.** The listing had been sitting for 11 days with no offers before Rachel introduced the candle staging. The buyers who made the offer specifically mentioned on their feedback form that the home "felt warm and lived-in, like somewhere we could see ourselves."

Rachel began tracking her metrics more carefully. Over the next six months, across 28 open houses, the data told a clear story.

**Average days on market dropped from 34 to 28 — an 18% reduction.** Rachel attributes this to the increased engagement she observed at open houses. When visitors spend more time in a home, they form stronger emotional connections. And the visit time data backed this up: average open house visits went from 12 minutes to 19 minutes after she introduced candle staging.

The most striking metric was in the feedback. **Before Ember & Oak, only 15% of buyer feedback forms mentioned the "feel" or "atmosphere" of the home. After, that number jumped to 47%.** Buyers were using words like "cozy," "warm," "welcoming," and "homey" — exactly the emotional responses that precede purchase decisions in residential real estate.

"I know it sounds crazy that candles helped me sell houses faster," Rachel said. "But atmosphere is everything in real estate. When someone walks into a home and it smells like warmth and comfort instead of paint and carpet cleaner, they start imagining living there. That's when they buy."

Financially, the investment was straightforward. Rachel now spends approximately $280 per month on Ember & Oak candles for staging (10-15 individual candles at $22 each, plus her personal Premium Box subscription). Her average commission on a home in her price range is roughly $12,000-$18,000. **One faster sale per quarter more than covers her entire annual candle investment.**

"My clients started asking me what candles I use at open houses," Rachel said. "That's when I knew it wasn't just me — the scent was actually making an impression. I've now recommended Ember & Oak to four other agents in my brokerage. Two of them are subscribers now."

## Key Takeaways

- **Atmosphere is a staging tool, not a luxury.** Most home staging focuses on visual presentation — furniture, lighting, decor. Rachel's experience demonstrates that scent is a powerful and underused dimension of staging that directly impacts how long buyers spend in a home and how emotionally connected they feel to a space.

- **Consistency and subtlety matter more than intensity.** The reason Ember & Oak worked where other candles failed was not about being stronger — it was about being more consistent. A scent that stays subtle and true for 3+ hours creates a genuine atmosphere. A scent that fades, shifts, or overpowers breaks the illusion and reminds buyers they are in a staged environment.

- **Small, measurable investments can have outsized returns.** Rachel's $280/month candle investment — roughly $3,360 per year — pays for itself with a single faster sale. When the ROI is that clear, the question shifts from "can I afford this?" to "can I afford not to?"
